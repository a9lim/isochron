# Isochron

Isochron is an Earth-like planet on one globe. Plate tectonics shapes it, rivers wear it down, and its climate follows from the geography. A logarithmic dial runs it from hours a second to ten million years a second.

## Plates and the seafloor

The surface is divided into rigid plates, each turning about its own Euler pole. Where plates part, new oceanic crust forms at the ridge; where they close, the denser crust sinks, ocean under continent and old ocean under young; where two continents meet, the crust thickens and the plates eventually weld. Plates are driven by the pull of their sinking slabs and the push of their cooling seafloor, against drag on their bases, which continents make stronger. Old seafloor at a margin begins to sink there, and large plates split along old sutures.

Beneath the plates the mantle keeps a memory of the ocean floor that has sunk into it. The slabs sink through the lower mantle over a hundred and fifty million years, and the mantle flows toward where they are going down and rises over two broad, hot piles on the core, as Earth's does under the Pacific and southern Africa. The plates ride that flow as well as being pulled by their own slabs, so a plate with no trench of its own still moves, carried toward the subduction zones. The flow bends the surface too, lifting it by a few hundred metres over the piles and lowering it over the old slabs. Plumes rise from the piles' edges, where the great flood basalts erupt, and the hotspots drift slowly in the mantle's flow, which is why a chain of seamounts can bend. On today's Earth the slabs are those that Müller and others' reconstruction sends down over the last 200 million years.

The plates are rigid, but their crust isn't quite. Where a continent rifts, its edge stretches into the opening gap before the ocean breaks through, so the margin thins into a slope between shelf and deep sea. Where continents collide, the crust thickens behind the front as a thrust wedge does, no steeper than a few degrees, so a mountain belt widens as it grows; past the thickness of Tibet's crust it spreads into a plateau instead of rising. Rifting gives continents area as collisions take it, so they hold about two fifths of the surface, as Earth's do. Where continents collide the incoming one's lithosphere keeps sinking after the ocean it followed, so they keep closing until they weld. Where ocean sinks, the plate bends down into a trench and up over a low rise beyond it, deepest where the sinking floor is oldest.

New crust records the planet's magnetic field as it cools, and the field reverses at random intervals, so the seafloor carries symmetric stripes either side of each ridge, as wide as the spreading rate times each interval. Seafloor deepens as it ages and cools, with the square root of its age at first, then levelling off.

## Sea level and the landscape

Elevation comes from isostasy: thicker and lighter crust floats higher. Sea level is where the ocean basins hold the planet's water, so fast spreading's young, shallow seafloor raises the sea and ice lowers it. The ocean is only what the open sea reaches: a basin a sill closes off is land, holding a lake as far as its own rain and rivers fill it, or lying dry below sea level as the Caspian, the Dead Sea and the Qattara Depression do. (A basin floored by the ocean's own crust stays sea.) Straits narrower than a cell join their seas while the land beside them stands higher: on today's Earth each place's channel comes from the measured seafloor, so Gibraltar, Hormuz and the Danish straits stay open, and the sea's currents keep a strait swept, cutting it down as fast as slow uplift raises it. A sill rising faster cuts its basin off with the water inside, and an evaporating sea dries to salt, as the Mediterranean did about six million years ago; the sea rising over a sill pours into the basin beyond. Water held in lakes is water out of the sea.

Rivers drain the land toward the sea, filling closed basins into lakes. Water runs at each place's lowest ground, so on today's Earth a river leaves a basin through a gorge narrower than a cell. A lake with no outlet settles where its evaporation balances what flows in, and slowed to the seasons it fills in the wet season and shrinks in the dry. A river cuts its bed faster with more discharge and a steeper slope, so mountains wear down from their rivers inward; the crust rebounds under what is removed, so old mountain belts outlast their erosion. The sediment fills lakes and builds shelves, and enough of it turns ocean crust into continent. Thick crust spreads under its own weight. Over hundreds of millions of years the ocean's volume drifts so the continents keep their height above the sea.

## Climate

Temperature comes from an energy balance: sunlight absorbed, less heat radiated to space, plus heat carried from warm latitudes to cold ones. Radiated heat falls as carbon dioxide rises, logarithmically. The ocean's surface layer stores heat through the year and land little, so continental interiors have wide seasons and coasts mild ones, and the seasons lag the sun more at sea. The winds carry heat as well as spreading it: the westerlies take an ocean's warmth and its mild seasons onto the continent downwind, not the one behind, and the seasons' winds carry a winter continent's cold where they blow. Snow and sea ice reflect sunlight, and the cold they keep makes more of them. Air is colder with height, so mountains carry snow.

Clouds come from the climate's own air. Where it rains, tall clouds reflect sunlight and hold in heat, since their tops are cold. Humid air carries layered clouds, and over seas colder than the air aloft, under the subtropical highs' sinking air, a warm layer caps a deck of stratocumulus. Over snow and ice a cloud reflects little more than the ground, so there clouds cool little and can warm. A warmer surface lifts the tall clouds' tops without warming them, so they hold in more of its heat.

Sunlight at each latitude follows from the planet's tilt, its orbit's eccentricity and where it is in that orbit at each solstice; the calendar is tied to the spring equinox.

The circulation follows a template rather than solving the atmosphere's equations. Rain gathers in a band (the intertropical convergence zone) that follows the warmest latitudes through the year. Dry air sinks in the subtropics at the edge of the Hadley cells, whose width grows with the equator-to-pole contrast and narrows as the planet spins faster; on Earth the subtropical highs stand near 30° and the subpolar lows near 60°, and the westerlies blow between them. The highs stand over the oceans: on their eastern sides the air runs toward the equator and sinks, over the deserts and dry west coasts, while on their western sides it runs poleward and the east coasts get the sea's rain. Land warmer than its latitude draws air in and land colder pushes it out, which makes monsoons and winter highs. Surface winds turn toward the low by friction.

Water evaporates from the sea, faster where it's warm and windy, and travels with the winds. Rain rises steeply with the air's humidity, so it falls where moist air converges, cools on its way poleward or is lifted up a slope; storms need a warm surface and drizzle needs air near saturation, so a hot desert's air, holding much water far from saturation, stays dry. The lee of a range lies in its rain shadow. Land gives back to the air the water its soils and plants evaporate, and that water rains again downwind, so a continent's interior lives partly on its own evaporation. The storm tracks' eddies spread moisture poleward; in the tropics the trade winds and monsoons carry it.

Past a point a warming planet runs away. The water vapour in a warm column grows opaque, so the heat the planet sends to space levels off near 282 W/m² however warm the surface. Once the sunlight it absorbs and the forcing pass that, no temperature balances them, and the excess boils the sea; the surface sits at the boiling point of the steam above it, 100 °C at a bar, past 300 °C at a hundred, and over 1,200 °C once the whole ocean is in the air. The sea level falls, the steam veils the globe, and the steam rains back if the forcing falls.

## Climate zones

The zones follow Köppen's classes, thresholds on monthly temperature and rain: tropical (A), arid (B), temperate (C), continental (D) and polar (E), with a second letter for when the dry season falls. Each threshold here is soft, standing for the variation from year to year, so each place holds a mixture of classes, drawn as a mosaic: the boundary between two climates is a band where both appear.

## Ice and the orbit

Where snow outlasts the summer it builds ice sheets, which flow outward under their own weight, faster where thick and steep. Snow falls where the month is below freezing at the ice's surface and melts by how far and how long the days rise above it, so a high sheet keeps itself cold. Ice presses the crust down, and it rises again thousands of years after the ice melts. The ice's water is taken from the sea.

The orbit changes in slow cycles: its eccentricity over about 100 and 400 thousand years, its tilt over about 41 thousand, and the season of its closest approach to the sun over about 22 thousand. Together they change how much sunlight summer brings to high latitudes, which decides whether winter snow survives. Fast, the globe shows the climate of the average orbit; slowed to thousands of years a second, the cycles come through.

## Carbon and plumes

Volcanoes at ridges and above sinking slabs release carbon dioxide, and weathering on land takes it back into the seafloor's sediments. Weathering runs faster where it's warm and wet and where erosion exposes fresh rock, so a warmer planet draws down its carbon dioxide and cools: a thermostat over hundreds of thousands of years. Faster plates outgas more and warm the planet; new mountains weather more and cool it. Plankton and plant debris buried at the coasts take carbon out of the air for good, and old organic carbon gives it back as erosion exposes it.

Columns of hot mantle rise beneath the plates at fixed points, building chains of seamounts and islands on whatever passes, oldest farthest away, and lifting the surface around them. Every few tens of millions of years a new plume's head arrives: a flood of basalt a thousand kilometres wide, a pulse of carbon dioxide and, under a continent, the rift that splits it.

## The ocean

The winds drive the ocean's surface into gyres: the open ocean drifts toward the equator or the pole as the wind's twisting requires and returns in a narrow, fast current along each basin's western shore. Warm water runs poleward on the west of each basin and cold water equatorward on the east, and where the wind pushes water off a coast, cold water wells up. Cold coasts evaporate and rain little, which makes coastal deserts.

The ocean carries heat. Its water keeps its own temperature as the currents carry it and gives heat to the air slowly, hardly at all through sea ice, so a current stays warm far from where it warmed. Below the gyres an overturning carries water up one hemisphere's oceans to sink in the other's: where a sea runs all the way round the planet, as the Southern Ocean does through Drake Passage, the westerlies drive its surface water toward the equator with nothing to return it, and it wells up there and sinks in the opposite hemisphere's deep subpolar seas. On Earth that is the Atlantic's overturning, carrying heat north across the equator to give it up over the Nordic Seas. Where its warm water lies under the surface, winter's mixing brings the heat up, so the sea stays open where the air alone would freeze it.

## Weather

Slowed to days a second, the weather runs live: two layers of air turning with the planet, whose difference is the temperature contrast between the equator and the poles. The contrast breaks into cyclones and anticyclones that carry heat poleward and stretch the surface temperature into fronts. Warm sea air rains out as it rises; where dry air from a high desert meets moist air from a warm sea, a dry line forms. Faster than the weather can run, the chart blurs into the climate's mean pressure and rain, which is what weather averages to.

The planet runs on cells about 55 km across (twice that on phones and tablets, refined for drawing). The weather runs on the graphics card, a step every 200 seconds, finding the air's flow from its spin by multigrid; much of the planet runs there too (the plates, the ice, the energy balance, the moisture, the tide, the ground and the dust), and the rest on a separate thread, so the globe stays smooth. Before the opening, the planet runs 80 million years from rest, so its plates have settled into their own pace. Its continents then stand as today's Earth's do, in the order that run left them: at each distance from the coast, the same shares of shelf, lowland, upland and mountain as Earth's. The sea is filled to stand as far above the ridges as Earth's does, so its oceans are as deep and its land covers about as much as Earth's.

## Tides and the Moon

The Moon raises a bulge in the sea that the planet turns under twice a day. The ocean can't keep up: the tide runs round each basin as a wave, turning about points where the sea barely rises and falls (amphidromic points), and where a basin's own period of sloshing is near the tide's it resonates, to ten metres or more. The tide is solved as the shallow-water equations on the rotating sphere, forced by the Moon and the Sun, and re-solved as the continents move.

The tide loses its energy to friction on the shelves and to waves breaking inside the ocean, and that energy comes out of the planet's spin: the day lengthens and the Moon moves slowly away, faster or slower as the plates rearrange the basins and their resonances. The planet starts with Earth's Moon and day, and the day's length feeds back into the climate, the gyres and the weather through the turning that steers the winds and currents.

Behind the globe is the sky as a star chart. At the slowest settings it turns once a day, and the Sun and the Moon appear where they are: the Sun along the ecliptic through the year, with spots that come and go through an eleven-year cycle and cross its face as it turns; the Moon round once a month, lit on the side facing the Sun, larger when nearer, its maria on the face it keeps turned to the planet.

## On the globe

Depth is shaded in blue and height in brown, with a contour every kilometre. Plate boundaries follow a tectonic map's conventions: trenches have sawteeth on the plate that stays on top, with red marks for the volcanic arc; where continents collide and nothing sinks, the teeth are open, as on a thrust belt; ridges are red; transform faults carry paired half-arrows; boundaries where the plates barely move are dotted. Hotspots are red rings, and a province erupting is a red stippled disc.

Land is coloured by its vegetation, from forest, savanna and steppe to desert, boreal forest and tundra, with bare rock toward the summits. Fire and grazing herds hold grassland open where the year has a warm dry season, so savanna and forest can each hold under one climate. Rivers are blue lines as heavy as their discharge; slowed to about a year a second they follow the seasons, and a river that runs dry is a dotted bed. Slowed to a few hours a second, where a day passes in a few seconds, every map shows the night side, darker in three steps as the Sun sinks 6°, 12° and more below the horizon (civil, nautical and astronomical twilight), and with people their lights there. Snow and sea ice are a light fill with a hard edge; sea ice follows the sea's own surface, so warm currents keep water open poleward. Ice sheets are outlined, with surface contours every 500 metres. Permafrost is hatched in violet in patches, whole where all the ground is frozen and scattered where only some is; snow insulates the ground, so permafrost reaches farther under thin snow. A dried closed lake is a salt flat, a pale crust under short grey strokes. The sea turns a milky teal where its plankton bloom, at upwelling coasts, river mouths and in the cold sea, and lakes bloom the same way: richest where warm, closed and fed phosphorus by their rivers' weathered rock, poorest where cold or flushed.

In the Zones map each class is its group's colour in a pattern for its second letter: solid where it's wet all year, hatched one way or the other where the dry season falls in winter or summer, cross-hatched for monsoon climates, stippled for deserts (densely) and steppes (sparsely), finely ruled for tundra, and bare for ice caps; the sea carries isotherms of its surface, the gyres' streamlines and green strokes where its plankton grow, and lakes are outlined and stroked the same way. The key lists each class with its share of the land.

The Air map shows the winds near the ground as evenly spaced streamlines with arrowheads downwind, heavier where stronger: the trade winds, the westerlies and the polar easterlies, air turning out of the highs and into the lows, and, slowed to the seasons, the monsoons reversing. Dust lifted from deserts, dry lake beds and the plains before the ice is an ochre stipple. After a large eruption its veil of sulfate is ruled along the circles of latitude, spreading and clearing over a year or two; the eruptions are red triangles.

The Weather view's pressure map is a synoptic chart: isobars every 4 hPa, rain as stipple, cold fronts with triangles and warm fronts with semicircles pointing the way they move, occluded fronts with both on one side, troughs dashed, ridges as zigzags, shear lines dash-dotted, dry lines with scallops toward the moist air, and the highs and lows. Its other maps show the surface temperature (isotherms every 5 °C), the air's humidity, and the tides as a co-tidal chart: the sea tinted by the tide's range, lines joining places with high water at the same lunar hour, converging on the amphidromic points, and stipple where the tide's energy is lost; slowed to hours a second, the sea's height now, with a heavier line at high water.

## Scenarios

Two scenarios start from the opening planet, picked beside the caption: World, the planet on its own, and People, which settles the climate as it loads and starts when the first hominins appear. A planet is drawn from a seed the link carries, so a link opens the same planet; New planet draws another. The caption gives the year, the date when slowed to the seasons, and with people how many there are and the largest city. The views come in three groups by timescale: Relief (the surface, the zones and the air), Weather (its charts and the tides, open only at a few days a second or slower) and People. The key at the globe's corner names each view's symbols. Drag to turn the globe, scroll to zoom, and set the rate with the dial.

## Today's Earth

The seed `earth` opens on today's Earth. Its relief is the bedrock of NOAA's ETOPO 2022 with Antarctica's and Greenland's grounded ice, and each place's lowest ground, where its rivers run through gorges finer than the globe's cells; the seafloor's ages are Seton and others' (2020); the plates and their motions are Müller and others' (2019) model of the present, with plates across diffuse boundaries (India, Capricorn and Australia among them) riding as one; the ten hotspots come from Whittaker and others' (2015) compilation; the magnetic stripes follow the GTS2012 timescale (Ogg 2012, as PmagPy tabulates it); and the orbit is fitted to Berger and Loutre's (1991) solution. The climate is tuned to Earth's: its temperature, rain and climate zones against the observed (the University of Delaware's land temperatures and rain, NOAA's sea surface temperatures and sea ice, GPCP's rain), and its sunlight reflected and heat radiated to space, with and without clouds, against NASA's CERES EBAF measurements (Loeb and others 2018). The crust is solved so its isostasy holds every place at its measured height, and the sea is filled to today's level. From then on everything is the model's own, so the planet shows the model's habits as well as Earth's shape. It opens at half a year a second, so the seasons cross the continents; the People scenario runs on it too.

## People

The People scenario places the first hominins once the climate has settled, at the heart of the broadest mix of trees and grass on the landmass that can hold most of them, and runs them to an ending. They spread by births and deaths over the land the climate and the plants allow, onto colder land as their culture grows, and cross the sea by voyages that found a people on an empty shore, a chance that falls with the crossing's length and rises with culture. They split into lineages where their range breaks apart; lineages that meet interbreed and merge, less the longer they have been apart, so a long-separated kind is replaced by an abler one rather than joined. They gather culture from the minds within reach: more people make more ideas, and ideas let the land carry more people (Kremer's loop), so their growth is hyperbolic. Farming begins where settled people meet plants to domesticate (in seasonally dry climates, and on rivers' floodplains in dry lands) and no crops at hand suit the place, so it begins in many places; crops spread to climates like the one they came from, and farmers breed them to new ones. Fields take a river's floodplain first, watered and renewed by its floods and cropped as often as the season allows, then land whose growing season is long enough. Where the rain has leached the soil the fields lie fallow most years, and in the warm, wet tropics disease thins the farmers, until industry brings fertilizer and medicine. Ships cross oceans only late. Industry begins where culture, farms, coal and ore meet, spreads over two centuries, and runs one world economy of capital, energy and materials. Solar farms take sunny land, open land before fields; fields no one works go back to the wild, and nothing grows past the heat that kills every plant. Computing comes to do people's work as its capability grows, at today's scale when it begins (a percent of investment, ~50 GW), and the loop closes again without the demographic transition to brake it; the dial runs out where the minds double in under a day. At early takeoff the ending is drawn by chance on two axes: whether the machines are aligned with people, and whether Earth is kept or spent. The planet answers throughout: fields replace forest, tillage strips soil, burned carbon enters the air and the sea, waste heat and sulfate force the climate, and past a runaway greenhouse the oceans boil away. The dial follows the people, each doubling or halving of what changes fastest taking about ten seconds, until it is taken by hand; Follow gives it back.

The People view has three maps. Settlement shows the land in the surface's colours where people have lived and grey where no one has yet, so their spread reads as land coming into colour. Density shows people as a tint of red, deeper where denser, and robots at work as a tint of green. Farmers mostly live on the land, and with industry most people live in cities, which grow where they can draw on the most people, best at ports and on rivers, the largest on a whole country. On both maps the land is divided among its uses in patches that stay put as the globe zooms: blocks where people and machines live (cities in red, datacenters in green), parcels for fields (farms in green-brown, solar farms in blue) and factories hatched heavily, each taking the land its people or power need. Robots come after the minds that drive them, since they and their factories must be built, and power plants take people's or robots' hands to build, so the land fills only as fast as the robots come. Coal's sulfate is a haze over industry and downwind of it; sulfate sprayed high to hold the climate spreads round the globe. Lights shows the globe at night, lit by the light people make, as satellite images of night lights show Earth: ochre dots, closer where brighter, merging where it is brightest. Every person's hearths and lamps give a little, about a candle's worth, so foragers' land shows a sparse scatter and farmers' land is lit by how densely they live; with industry, a fixed share of the power each person's goods take goes to electric light, which grows more efficient as culture grows, from gas mantles' and filaments' toward LEDs', so industry's countryside is lit densely and its cities solidly. Machines work unlit: an Earth its people leave goes dark, and a people who lose their share of the economy lose its light. In the sky the Moon's city and the habitats beside the Moon are lit too, and the swarm's collectors carry the Sun's light. On Earth the robots do what its own economy needs, in its cities, fields and factories; the rest go off-world. There the swarm of collectors round the Sun is as large as its material allows: the innermost planet (Mercury, on today's Earth) is taken apart for it, until the Sun's output caps it. Where Earth is spent the swarm shades it and dims the Sun; where Earth is kept, it keeps out of Earth's light. A city spreads across the Moon's near side, and habitats gather either side of the Moon on its orbit. If the machines are aligned, people settle the worlds round the Sun as each opens, nearer and sunnier first (on today's Earth the Moon, Mars, Ceres and the moons of Jupiter and Saturn), and habitats round the Sun built from the asteroid belt. Only today's Earth has today's Solar System: every other planet has its own, its planets, belt and moons drawn by chance about the same Sun, and none of them named. Fertility is heritable, so where there is room it rises generation by generation toward a frontier's, and their numbers grow by orders of magnitude until the belt runs out; a whole Earth fills too, toward a planet-wide city with the factories and datacenters its own people need. Once the swarm takes a hundredth of the Sun, probes leave for the stars at half the speed of light, and a star lights up in the sky once the light of its settlement could have come back; each settled star's people grow as the Sun's did, on their own.

## The margin

The figures are drawn from the same state. The Model tab, open first, draws each running layer as a node with its live value and each coupling as an arrow as heavy as what flows along it, so the weather appears only in its view and the year's weather only at the dials where it steps. With people it draws their economy, growing as its eras arrive: land, people, minds and culture; then farms; then industry's output, made from work, energy and ores, part kept as capital and the rest invested in energy, computing and robots; computing thinking beside people's minds and working beside people, and the robots it drives; the climate, the air and space at its edge, space giving sunlight's power and, once mined, materials. An arrow of alignment runs from people to computing, as heavy as the control people keep; if the machines turn out misaligned, computing and robots are named in red. The Climate tab has temperature and rain by latitude, the rain belt's sweep, the Hadley cells' edges and the zones' shares; the Crust tab the surface's heights and depths and the reversals; the Record tab the planet's temperature, carbon dioxide, sea level, ice, land and ridge activity on a time axis that follows the dial, and the orbit's cycles with July sunlight at 65° N; and with people a People tab plotting people and minds against the time left to the singularity their growth points at, the lineages below as bands, and, once anything is off-world, the planet's solar system: the swarm round the Sun, the belt and the innermost planet as they are mined, and the people at home and on each world they live on.
